So why would a CPU not be fully utilized, even if it appears to be at 100%? The answer is usually memory. Memory accesses are orders of magnitude slower than internal CPU operations. To give a good picture of how much so, a modern CPU might be able to process somewhere in the realm of 4-8 instructions per nanosecond. A memory access to RAM can take somewhere in the realm of 50-100ns. To ease your mathing, that means a modern CPU can do around 400-800 instructions for every one memory access from RAM. Given this, CPUs are built from the ground up to work their way around this. The most obvious way this is dealt with is CPU caches. CPU caches keep memory closer to the CPU than RAM, literally baked into the CPU chip. The closest cache (the L1 cache) takes around 1ns per memory operation- that's 50-100x faster than RAM! The "problem" here of course is that the CPU caches are fairly small; the L1 cache for a modern CPU is probably around ~64KB, while the farther caches max out at a few hundred MB for top end processors. A good database tries manipulating data in small "chunks" to take full advantage of caches, and you'll often hear cache utilization as being one of the top concerns of performance engineering.
